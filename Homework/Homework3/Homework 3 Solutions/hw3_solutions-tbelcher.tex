   \item \emph{(Question 1.)} Gradient propagation and CNN dimensions.
   \begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\Alph*.}, leftmargin=*]
   \item \emph{Gradient propagation.} We have a scalar network with $L$ layers with the initial input $h_0 = x$ and at each subsequent layer $l$ (where $1 \leq l \leq L$), $h_l = \sigma (w_lh_{l-1})$ and $\sigma(z) = \dfrac{1}{1+e^{-z}}$. $|w_l| \leq 1$ and there are no biases. 
   
   \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
    \item We want to estimate an upper bound on $\left|\dfrac{\partial h_L}{\partial x}\right|$ in terms of $L$. We have that 
    \[
    \begin{aligned}
        h_L &= \sigma(w_L h_{L-1}) \\
            &= \sigma\bigl(w_L \sigma\bigl(w_{L-1} \sigma(\cdots \sigma(w_1 x))\bigr)\bigr).
    \end{aligned}
    \]

    At any given level $l \geq 2$, let $g_l(x)$ denote the remaining nested expression, including the weight $w_l$, all the way down to the input $x$:
    \[
        g_l(x) = w_l \sigma\bigl(w_{l-1} \sigma(\cdots \sigma(w_1 x))\bigr),
        \qquad g_1(x) = w_1 x.
    \]
    Thus $h_l = \sigma(g_l(x))$. By the chain rule, the partial derivative of $h_L$ with respect to $x$ can be written as

    \[
    \begin{aligned}
        \left|\frac{\partial h_L}{\partial x}\right|
            &= \left|\sigma'(g_L(x))\,g_L'(x)\right| \\
            &= \left|\sigma'(g_L(x))\right|\,
               \left|w_L\,\sigma'(g_{L-1}(x))\,g_{L-1}'(x)\right| \\
            &= \left|\sigma'(g_L(x))\right|\,
               \left|w_L\,\sigma'(g_{L-1}(x))\right| \\
            &\quad {}\cdot \left|w_{L-1}\,\sigma'(g_{L-2}(x))\right|
               \cdots
               \left|w_2\,\sigma'(g_1(x))\right|\,|w_1| \\
            &= \left(\prod_{l=1}^{L}|w_l|\right)
               \left(\prod_{l=1}^{L}\left|\sigma'(g_l(x))\right|\right) \\
            &\leq 1\cdot \prod_{l=1}^{L}\left|\sigma'(g_l(x))\right|.
    \end{aligned}
    \]
    The inequality follows from $|w_l| \leq 1$ for every layer, so $\prod_{l=1}^{L}|w_l| \leq 1$. Now the derivative of the sigmoid activation function is
    \[
        \sigma'(z) = -(1+e^{-z})^{-2}(-e^{-z})
        = \frac{e^{-z}}{(1+e^{-z})^2}.
    \]
    To bound this derivative, observe that
    \[
        1-\sigma(z) = 1-\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}
        = \frac{e^{-z}}{1+e^{-z}}.
    \]
    Thus we can rewrite the derivative as
    \[
        \sigma'(z) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}\cdot\frac{e^{-z}}{1+e^{-z}}
        = \sigma(z)\bigl(1-\sigma(z)\bigr).
    \]
    Since $0<\sigma(z)<1$, this derivative is positive. Completing the square gives
    \[
        |\sigma'(z)| = \sigma(z)\bigl(1-\sigma(z)\bigr)
        = \frac14-\left(\sigma(z)-\frac12\right)^2
        \leq \frac14.
    \]
    Applying this bound to each of the $L$ factors in our product yields
    \[
    \begin{aligned}
        \left|\frac{\partial h_L}{\partial x}\right|
            &\leq \prod_{l=1}^{L}\left|\sigma'(g_l(x))\right| \\
            &\leq \prod_{l=1}^{L}\frac14 \\
            &= \boxed{\left(\frac14\right)^L = 4^{-L}}.
    \end{aligned}
    \]
    Therefore the magnitude of the input derivative is bounded above by $4^{-L}$, which decreases exponentially with the number of layers. Gradients propagated through many of these layers can become very small, making it difficult to train earlier layers. The bound describes gradient attenuation; it does not determine the training time.

    \item If the weights were not bounded in magnitude by 1, then a larger product of weight magnitudes could offset small sigmoid derivatives. However, larger weights can also increase the magnitude of the sigmoid inputs, moving them into saturation regions where the derivative is small. Increasing the weights can therefore help with a vanishing gradient, but it is not a guaranteed solution. More precisely, each layer contributes both a weight and a sigmoid derivative:
    \[
        \left|\frac{\partial h_l}{\partial h_{l-1}}\right|
        = \left|w_l\sigma'(w_lh_{l-1})\right|
        \leq \frac{|w_l|}{4}.
    \]
    With $|w_l|\leq 1$, each factor is at most $1/4$, giving the exponential bound in part (i). Without this weight bound, larger weights could offset small sigmoid derivatives, so small sigmoid derivatives alone do not prove that every deep network must have vanishing gradients. However, larger weights can also move the sigmoid inputs into regions where its derivative is small. Increasing the weights is therefore not a guaranteed solution.

    A vector residual block adds its input back to a learned transformation of that input:
    \[
        \mathbf h_{l+1}=\mathbf h_l+F_l(\mathbf h_l),
        \qquad F_l:\mathbb R^d\to\mathbb R^d.
    \]
    Its Jacobian contains the partial derivative of each output component with respect to each input component. Writing the $i$th output component as $h_{l+1,i}=h_{l,i}+F_{l,i}(\mathbf h_l)$ gives
    \[
    \begin{aligned}
        \relax[J_l]_{ij}
        &=\frac{\partial h_{l+1,i}}{\partial h_{l,j}} \\
        &=\frac{\partial h_{l,i}}{\partial h_{l,j}}
          +\frac{\partial F_{l,i}(\mathbf h_l)}{\partial h_{l,j}} \\
        &=\delta_{ij}+[J_{F_l}(\mathbf h_l)]_{ij},
    \end{aligned}
    \]
    where $\delta_{ij}=1$ if $i=j$ and $0$ otherwise, is Kronecker's delta. These entries form the identity matrix, so
    \[
        \boxed{J_l=I+J_{F_l}(\mathbf h_l)}.
    \]
    For a scalar loss $\mathcal L$, the chain rule propagates its gradient backward through the block as
    \[
        \nabla_{\mathbf h_l}\mathcal L
        =\bigl(I+J_{F_l}(\mathbf h_l)\bigr)^{\mathsf T}
         \nabla_{\mathbf h_{l+1}}\mathcal L.
    \]
    The identity term provides a direct contribution from the next layer's gradient. When $J_{F_l}$ is small, the block's Jacobian is close to $I$, which helps preserve gradients. This does not guarantee successful training: for example, $J_{F_l}=-I$ would cancel the identity term entirely, and products of Jacobians across many blocks can still shrink or amplify gradients.
   \end{enumerate} 

   \item \emph{CNN dimensions and parameter counts.}

   The input is $3\times24\times24$ in channel--height--width order.
   Convolutions have stride 1, no padding, dilation 1, and one bias per
   output channel. Pooling uses a $2\times2$ window with stride 2,
   no padding, and floor rounding.

   \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
    \item For either spatial dimension $n$, the output-size formula is
    \[
        n_{\mathrm{out}}
        =\left\lfloor\frac{n_{\mathrm{in}}+2p-d(k-1)-1}{s}\right\rfloor+1,
    \]
    where $k$ is the kernel size, $p$ is padding, $d$ is dilation,
    and $s$ is stride. Here $p=0$ and $d=1$, so this becomes
    $n_{\mathrm{out}}=\lfloor(n_{\mathrm{in}}-k)/s\rfloor+1$.
    Since height and width are equal, the same calculation applies to both:
    \begin{center}
    \begin{tabular}{l c c}
        \toprule
        Layer & Spatial calculation & Output dimension \\
        \midrule
        Conv 1 & $\lfloor(24-3)/1\rfloor+1=22$ & $12\times22\times22$ \\
        Pool 1 & $\lfloor(22-2)/2\rfloor+1=11$ & $12\times11\times11$ \\
        Conv 2 & $\lfloor(11-3)/1\rfloor+1=9$ & $128\times9\times9$ \\
        Pool 2 & $\lfloor(9-2)/2\rfloor+1=4$ & $128\times4\times4$ \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    \end{center}
    A convolution's channel count is its number of output filters, while
    pooling preserves the channel count. In Pool 2, floor rounding gives
    $4$, so the last row and column of the $9\times9$ input are not covered
    by a pooling window.

    \item Since groups $=1$, each convolutional output filter spans all input channels.
    Therefore a $k\times k$ filter has $C_{\mathrm{in}}k^2$ weights and
    one bias, giving
    \[
        N_{\mathrm{conv}}=C_{\mathrm{out}}\cdot(C_{\mathrm{in}}k^2+1).
    \]
    For Conv 1, each output filter has $3\cdot3\cdot3=27$ weights
    and one bias. With 12 output filters, the total is
    \[
        N_{\mathrm{Conv\,1}}=12(27+1)=\boxed{336}
        \quad\text{($324$ weights and $12$ biases).}
    \]
    Conv 2 receives 12 channels, so each output filter has
    $12\cdot3\cdot3=108$ weights and one bias. With 128 output filters,
    \[
        N_{\mathrm{Conv\,2}}=128(108+1)=\boxed{13{,}952}
        \quad\text{($13{,}824$ weights and $128$ biases).}
    \]
    Max pooling only selects the maximum within each window and has
    no trainable weights or biases. Thus Pool 1 and Pool 2 each have
    $\boxed{0}$ trainable parameters.

    \item After Pool 2, flattening the $128\times4\times4$ tensor gives
    \[
        n_{\mathrm{flat}}=128\cdot4\cdot4=\boxed{2{,}048}
    \]
    features. A fully connected layer with 10 outputs has one weight
    for each input--output pair and one bias per output, so it adds
    \[
        N_{\mathrm{FC}}=2{,}048\cdot10+10=\boxed{20{,}490}
    \]
    trainable parameters. Flattening itself has no trainable parameters.

    In a convolution, the same filter weights and bias are reused at
    every spatial location. Thus its parameter count depends on the
    kernel size and channel counts, rather than the number of output
    locations. A fully connected mapping learns separate weights for
    every input--output pair. For example, a fully connected mapping
    from the original $3\cdot24\cdot24=1{,}728$ inputs to the
    $12\cdot22\cdot22=5{,}808$ outputs of Conv 1 would require
    \[
        1{,}728\cdot5{,}808+5{,}808=10{,}042{,}032
    \]
    parameters, compared with Conv 1's 336. This reduction comes from
    both local connectivity (each output uses a $3\times3$ spatial patch)
    and weight sharing (all locations use the same filters).
   \end{enumerate} 

   \end{enumerate}

   \item \emph{(Question 2.)} We are given the following network and constraints. 

   For one example $(x,y)$, where $y\in\{0,1\}$, all four weights
   are scalars and there are no biases. The hidden activation $\phi$
   changes between parts, while the output activation is always sigmoid.

   \begin{center}
   \begin{tikzpicture}[
       neuron/.style={circle, draw, thick, minimum size=12mm},
       connection/.style={->, thick, >=stealth},
       every node/.style={font=\normalsize}
   ]

       \node[neuron] (hidden1) at (3,1.3) {$h_1$};
       \node[neuron] (hidden2) at (3,-1.3) {$h_2$};
       \node[neuron] (output) at (6,0) {$\hat y$};

       \draw[connection] (input) -- node[above, sloped] {$w_1$} (hidden1);
       \draw[connection] (input) -- node[below, sloped] {$w_2$} (hidden2);
       \draw[connection] (hidden1) -- node[above, sloped] {$w_3$} (output);
       \draw[connection] (hidden2) -- node[below, sloped] {$w_4$} (output);

       \node at (0,2.2) {Input};
       \node at (3,2.2) {Hidden activation: $\phi$};
       \node at (6,2.2) {Output: $\sigma$};
   \end{tikzpicture}

   The forward pass is
   \[
   \begin{aligned}
       h_1 &= \phi(w_1x), & h_2 &= \phi(w_2x), \\
       z &= w_3h_1+w_4h_2, & \hat y &= \sigma(z),
   \end{aligned}
   \]
   with binary cross-entropy loss
   \[
       \mathcal L=-y\log(\hat y)-(1-y)\log(1-\hat y).
   \]
   We use the natural logarithm for calculations.

   \begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\Alph*.}, leftmargin=*]
       \item Let $\phi=\sigma$. Then since $\hat{y} = \sigma(z)$, the partial derivative of the loss function $\mathcal L$ with respect to $z$ is:
       \[
       \begin{aligned}
           \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\hat y}
               &=-\frac{y}{\hat y}+\frac{1-y}{1-\hat y}, \\
           \frac{\partial\hat y}{\partial z}
               &=\hat y(1-\hat y), \\
           \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial z}
               &=\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\hat y}
                 \frac{\partial\hat y}{\partial z} \\
               &=\left(-\frac{y}{\hat y}+\frac{1-y}{1-\hat y}\right)
                 \hat y(1-\hat y) \\
               &=-y(1-\hat y)+(1-y)\hat y
                =\boxed{\hat y-y}.
       \end{aligned}
       \]
       We have that $z = w_3h_1 + w_4h_2$. Therefore: 
       \[
       \begin{aligned}
           \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial w_3}
               &=\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial z}
                 \frac{\partial z}{\partial w_3}
                =(\hat{y}-y)h_1 \\
               &=\boxed{\bigl[\sigma(w_3\sigma(w_1x)+w_4\sigma(w_2x))-y\bigr]
                         \sigma(w_1x)}, \\
           \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial w_4}
               &=\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial z}
                 \frac{\partial z}{\partial w_4}
                =(\hat{y}-y)h_2 \\
               &=\boxed{\bigl[\sigma(w_3\sigma(w_1x)+w_4\sigma(w_2x))-y\bigr]
                         \sigma(w_2x)}.
       \end{aligned}
       \]
       Let $a_1=w_1x$ and $a_2=w_2x$, with
       $h_1=\sigma(a_1)$ and $h_2=\sigma(a_2)$.
       \[
       \begin{aligned}
           \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial w_1}
               &=\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial z}
                 \frac{\partial z}{\partial h_1}
                 \frac{\partial h_1}{\partial a_1}
                 \frac{\partial a_1}{\partial w_1} \\
               &=(\hat{y}-y)\cdot w_3\cdot h_1(1-h_1)\cdot x
                =(\hat{y}-y)w_3h_1(1-h_1)x.
       \end{aligned}
       \]
       Therefore,
       \[
           \boxed{\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial w_1}
           =\bigl[\sigma(w_3\sigma(w_1x)+w_4\sigma(w_2x))-y\bigr]
             w_3\sigma(w_1x)\bigl(1-\sigma(w_1x)\bigr)x}.
       \]
       \[
       \begin{aligned}
           \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial w_2}
               &=\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial z}
                 \frac{\partial z}{\partial h_2}
                 \frac{\partial h_2}{\partial a_2}
                 \frac{\partial a_2}{\partial w_2} \\
               &=(\hat{y}-y)\cdot w_4\cdot h_2(1-h_2)\cdot x
                =(\hat{y}-y)w_4h_2(1-h_2)x.
       \end{aligned}
       \]
       Therefore,
       \[
           \boxed{\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial w_2}
           =\bigl[\sigma(w_3\sigma(w_1x)+w_4\sigma(w_2x))-y\bigr]
             w_4\sigma(w_2x)\bigl(1-\sigma(w_2x)\bigr)x}.
       \]


       With learning rate $\eta>0$, the simultaneous gradient-descent
       updates are
       \[
       \begin{aligned}
           w_1^{\mathrm{new}}&=w_1-\eta(\hat y-y)w_3h_1(1-h_1)x, \\
           w_2^{\mathrm{new}}&=w_2-\eta(\hat y-y)w_4h_2(1-h_2)x, \\
           w_3^{\mathrm{new}}&=w_3-\eta(\hat y-y)h_1, \\
           w_4^{\mathrm{new}}&=w_4-\eta(\hat y-y)h_2.
       \end{aligned}
       \]
       All quantities on the right, including $h_1$, $h_2$, and $\hat y$,
       are evaluated at the same pre-update weights.

       \item Let
       \[
           \phi(u)=\frac{\log(1+e^{2ku})}{2k},\qquad k>0.
       \]
       Differentiating gives
       \[
       \begin{aligned}
           \phi'(u)
               &=\frac{1}{2k}\cdot\frac{1}{1+e^{2ku}}
                 \cdot e^{2ku}\cdot2k \\
               &=\frac{e^{2ku}}{1+e^{2ku}}
                =\frac{1}{1+e^{-2ku}}
                =\boxed{\sigma(2ku)}.
       \end{aligned}
       \]
       Thus the derivative is sigmoid evaluated at $2ku$, while the
       hidden outputs remain $h_1=\phi(w_1x)$ and $h_2=\phi(w_2x)$.
       The output activation and loss are unchanged, so
       $\partial\mathcal L/\partial z=\hat y-y$. The weight gradients are
       \[
       \begin{aligned}
           \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial w_1}
               &=(\hat y-y)w_3\phi'(w_1x)x
                =\boxed{(\hat y-y)w_3\sigma(2kw_1x)x}, \\
           \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial w_2}
               &=(\hat y-y)w_4\phi'(w_2x)x
                =\boxed{(\hat y-y)w_4\sigma(2kw_2x)x}, \\
           \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial w_3}
               &=(\hat y-y)h_1
                =\boxed{(\hat y-y)\phi(w_1x)}, \\
           \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial w_4}
               &=(\hat y-y)h_2
                =\boxed{(\hat y-y)\phi(w_2x)},
       \end{aligned}
       \]
       where $\hat y=\sigma\bigl(w_3\phi(w_1x)+w_4\phi(w_2x)\bigr)$.
       Our chosen $\phi(u)$ approaches ReLU as $k$ goes to infinity.
       For fixed $u>0$, the limit has the indeterminate form
       $\infty/\infty$. Applying L'H\^opital's rule with respect to $k$ gives
       \[
       \begin{aligned}
           \lim_{k\to\infty}\phi(u)
               &=\lim_{k\to\infty}
                 \frac{\log(1+e^{2ku})}{2k} \\
               &=\lim_{k\to\infty}
                 \frac{2u e^{2ku}/(1+e^{2ku})}{2} \\
               &=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{u}{1+e^{-2ku}}
                =u.
       \end{aligned}
       \]
       For fixed $u<0$, $e^{2ku}\to0$, so
       \[
           \lim_{k\to\infty}\phi(u)
           =\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\log(1+e^{2ku})}{2k}=0.
       \]
       At $u=0$,
       \[
           \lim_{k\to\infty}\phi(0)
           =\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\log2}{2k}=0.
       \]
       Therefore,
       \[
           \boxed{\lim_{k\to\infty}\phi(u)
           =\begin{cases}
               u, & u>0, \\
               0, & u\leq0
           \end{cases}
           =\max(0,u)=\operatorname{ReLU}(u)}.
       \]

       ReLU is differentiable for $u\neq0$, with derivative 0 for
       $u<0$ and 1 for $u>0$. At $u=0$, its ordinary derivative does
       not exist, but we can choose a value for the purposes of backpropogation. Since $u=0$ is the boundary between a zero and non-zero derivative, a choice of $0$ for the derivative makes sense, but we could choose another value between $0$ and $1$ instead. This supplies a value
       for the chain-rule factor when a unit's input is exactly zero,
       allowing backpropagation to proceed. Choosing 1 is also valid,
       but does not change the zero derivative for negative inputs.
       Thus the smooth activation $\phi$ is an alternative to ReLU,
       rather than a prerequisite for training with ReLU.

   \item \emph{(Question 3.)} Evaluation protocol audit.
   \begin{enumerate}[(a)]
       \item The preprocessing leakage occurs because the student
       includes the test observations when computing the means and
       standard deviations used for standardization. These parameters
       should be estimated from the training set only, then applied
       to the validation and test sets. Otherwise, information from
       the test set influences the model's preprocessing.

       Additionally, the student uses the test set to choose the best
       of 20 architectures. Test scores depend partly on the particular
       observations in the test set, so selecting the highest score
       can favor a model that performs unusually well on those
       observations by chance. The selected score is consequently
       optimistic as an estimate of performance on new data.
       Repeating the procedure five times does not remove either
       problem: each repetition still leaks test information and
       selects a model using its test performance.

       \item We should first split the data into training, validation,
       and test sets, for example using an $80/10/10$ split. Fit the
       standardization parameters on the training set only and use
       those same parameters to transform all three sets. Train the
       candidate models on the training set and use validation
       performance to tune hyperparameters and select the architecture.

       For early stopping, monitor validation loss during training.
       Stop when it has not improved for a prespecified number of
       epochs, and restore the checkpoint with the
       lowest validation loss. This selects the training duration
       using validation data rather than test data. After choosing
       the model with the best validation performance and fixing all
       modeling choices, evaluate it on the held-out test set and
       report its performance without using that result for further
       tuning or selection.

       \item If several rows measure the same material, placing those
       rows in both training and testing can make test performance
       optimistic for prediction on previously unseen materials.
       The model may benefit from having already seen related
       measurements of a test material during training.
       We should instead split by material: all rows from a given
       material must belong entirely to the training, validation,
       or test set. This also prevents validation-based selection
       from benefiting from overlap with training materials.
       We need a material identifier for each row that reliably
       groups repeated measurements of the same material, including
       records that use different names for that material.
   \end{enumerate}

       \item \emph{Interpretation and assessment.}
       The results support my prediction that an MLP would outperform
       ridge under this evaluation. Mean RMSE was $17.632$ K for ridge
       and $11.208$ K for the base MLP, a paired reduction of $6.424$ K.
       Mean $R^2$ increased from $0.73795$ to $0.89409$.
       The depth prediction was also supported: mean RMSE fell from
       $12.948$ K with one hidden layer to $11.208$ K with two, then
       to $10.989$ K with three. The improvement from the third layer
       was smaller and varied across splits, ranging from $0.024$ to
       $0.353$ K relative to the two-layer reference.
       Selected validation MSE improved with added depth in every split,
       alongside lower training MSE, so the change was not confined to
       fitting the training data.

       Our strongest supported conclusion is that these MLPs predicted
       held-out rows more accurately than ridge, and added layers helped
       under the declared training settings. This is consistent with
       useful nonlinear relationships, but does not identify a particular
       relationship between material properties and temperature.
       An alternative explanation for the depth improvement is the
       additional parameters rather than depth itself; optimization and
       different stopping epochs may also contribute. We propose a next
       experiment comparing different depths with adjusted widths to
       give similar parameter counts, while retaining common splits and
       training rules. That would help distinguish a depth benefit from
       the benefit of a larger parameter budget. It was not run here.

       Random row splits do not establish generalization to previously
       unseen material families. In seeds 11, 22, and 33, respectively,
       1,485, 1,497, and 1,477 test rows had a feature vector also present
       in training. These exact feature matches are a concrete overlap
       diagnostic, though they are not verified material identifiers.
       Repeated or related materials can occur in multiple partitions,
       allowing performance on familiar materials to contribute to the
       test score. A stronger evaluation would group rows by material
       or family before splitting. It would require material identifiers,
       chemical formulas, and family groupings, which are not supplied
       in this numerical feature file.
